Peptide therapy is not one standard treatment, and evidence, risks, and regulatory status can differ from one product to another. That makes a thoughtful conversation with a qualified clinician especially important. What to expect during a peptide therapy consultation includes a review of your health history, the concern you want to address, and any medications or supplements you take. You can ask what research supports the specific peptide, what remains uncertain, and whether other approaches may suit you better. The clinician may request records or recommend further evaluation before discussing treatment. Preparing a short list of goals and questions can help you make the most of the appointment without feeling you need to choose a treatment in advance.

## Key Takeaways

* **Ask about the specific peptide and its intended use**: Evidence, potential benefits, risks, and regulatory status vary by product, so discuss the details with a clinician.
* **Prepare a clear picture of your health**: Bring your diagnoses, allergies, medications, supplements, past treatments, symptoms, goals, and any recent lab results. Let your clinician decide whether more testing is needed.
* **Use the consultation to plan your next step**: Discuss alternatives, costs, monitoring, follow-up, and whether telehealth is suitable for your needs. A consultation may lead to treatment, further evaluation, or another approach.

## What Is Peptide Therapy?

### Peptides are short amino acid chains with effects that vary by type

Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. The body uses different peptides to help regulate processes such as hormone activity, immune responses, inflammation, digestion, and tissue repair. In medicine, a peptide may be considered for a specific purpose, but “peptide therapy” doesn’t describe one standard treatment with one predictable effect. The peptide, how it’s used, and a person’s health all matter. As [Harvard Health explains](https://www.health.harvard.edu/medications-and-treatments/peptides-what-they-are-potential-benefits-and-safety-concerns), peptides can have many different roles in the body. So, when you hear a claim about peptide therapy, ask which specific peptide it refers to and what it is intended to do.

### Each peptide has its own uses, evidence, risks, and regulatory status

Evidence and safety information for one peptide may not apply to another. Ask what research supports the specific peptide for its intended use, how strong that evidence is, and what questions remain unanswered. You’ll also want to discuss possible side effects, medication interactions, and reasons the treatment may not be suitable for you. Regulatory status is another important part of the conversation. Some peptide medications are FDA-approved for specific uses; others may be compounded. Compounded medications are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review them for safety and effectiveness before they are marketed. The agency explains [how compounded drugs are regulated](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers). Ask your clinician what a product’s status means for your care.

### Peptides differ from steroids and aren’t interchangeable

Peptides and anabolic steroids are different types of substances. Peptides are short chains of amino acids, while anabolic steroids are typically synthetic versions of hormones such as testosterone. They can act differently in the body, have different potential risks, and serve different medical purposes. Neither should be treated as a substitute for the other, and advice about using one doesn’t apply to the other. If you’re considering peptide therapy because you’ve heard it compared with testosterone or steroids, bring that up with your clinician. [Harvard Health’s overview of peptides](https://www.health.harvard.edu/medications-and-treatments/peptides-what-they-are-potential-benefits-and-safety-concerns) outlines the basic distinction. Your provider can explain the intended use of a specific treatment and whether it relates to your health needs.

### Peptides don’t guarantee muscle growth, fat loss, recovery, or other outcomes

Claims about faster recovery, muscle growth, fat loss, or athletic performance can sound appealing, especially when they come from a training partner or social media. But peptide therapy can’t guarantee those results. Research may be limited, and findings for one peptide or group of people don’t necessarily predict what will happen for you. Outcomes can depend on the specific product, the reason for use, your health, and other factors such as training, sleep, and nutrition. Be cautious of broad promises or claims that a peptide works for everyone. Ask what evidence supports the outcome you’re interested in, how progress would be assessed, and what other approaches may be worth considering.

### Ask a clinician whether a treatment fits your health history and goals

Before discussing a peptide, a clinician should review your health history, symptoms, medications, supplements, and goals. Share relevant allergies, past treatments, and any products you’ve already tried. Ask about potential benefits and risks, alternatives, monitoring, and what to do if you notice a side effect. You can also ask how strong the evidence is for the proposed use and whether you need further evaluation first. This is especially important in telehealth, where the clinician needs enough information to determine whether remote care is appropriate for your situation. A [peptide consultation checklist](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) can help you prepare questions. The conversation may lead to treatment, further evaluation, another approach, or a decision not to use a peptide.

## What Should You Prepare for a Peptide Therapy Consultation?

A little preparation can help your clinician understand your health history and make the appointment more focused. You don’t need to arrive with every answer or order tests on your own. Gather the information you already have, make a note of what you’d like to discuss, and ask your clinician if anything else would be useful.

### Gather your diagnoses, allergies, surgeries, procedures, and relevant records

Make a list of current and past diagnoses, known allergies, surgeries, and medical procedures. If you have relevant records, such as specialist notes or discharge summaries, bring them or ask how to share them securely. This information can help your clinician understand your health history and decide whether a particular treatment is appropriate to consider.

For allergies, note what caused the reaction and what happened, if you remember. Include details even if you’re unsure they matter. Your clinician can determine what is relevant and whether more information is needed. A [complete medical history](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) can support a more informed discussion.

### List your medications, supplements, over-the-counter products, and past peptide use

Write down the prescription medications, supplements, vitamins, and over-the-counter products you currently use. Include the dose and how often you take each one, if you know. Also note any peptide therapies you’ve tried before, when you used them, and whether you experienced side effects or other changes.

Share the full list, even if a product seems unrelated to your reason for booking. Your clinician can review it alongside your health history and discuss whether any items may affect treatment considerations. If you’re unsure of a product’s name or dose, bring the package or a photo of its label. This [guide to discussing peptides with a doctor](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) also recommends sharing current products and past peptide use.

### Bring recent lab results, but don’t order tests unless asked

If you have recent lab results, bring a copy or ask how to send them securely before your appointment. Your clinician can determine whether they’re relevant to your symptoms and health history, and whether additional evaluation may be appropriate.

You don’t need to arrange new tests just to prepare. Testing depends on your individual circumstances and the treatment being considered, so ask your clinician before booking anything. If you’re unsure whether a result is recent or useful, bring it and ask. The clinician can explain what it shows and what it cannot establish. [Peptide consultation guidance](https://innovative-care.com/peptide-consultation) recommends bringing existing lab results and waiting for the clinician to request further testing.

### Note your symptoms, goals, routine, and concerns

Before the appointment, write down what prompted you to ask about peptide therapy. Note when your symptoms began, how often they occur, and whether anything seems to affect them. Describe your goals in practical terms, such as the health concern you’d like to address. Relevant details about your daily routine may also help your clinician understand your needs.

List any concerns or questions, including those about potential risks, cost, or how a treatment might fit into your life. You don’t need to know which treatment is right for you. Your notes can help guide a discussion about your options and whether further evaluation is needed. Preparing these details is one way to make the conversation more productive, as [doctor discussion guidance](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) recommends.

### Write down where you heard about peptide therapy and which claims you want to discuss

Note how you first heard about peptide therapy, whether through a trainer, friend, social media, or another source. If a specific claim caught your attention, write it down and bring the link or material where you saw it. This helps your clinician respond to the information you encountered and explain what is known, what remains uncertain, and what may apply to your situation.

You can also list questions about a treatment or product you’re considering. You don’t need to decide beforehand whether a claim is reliable. Sharing its source gives your clinician a chance to address it directly and keep the discussion focused on your health history, goals, and options.

## What Happens During the Consultation?

### Review your health history, concerns, and treatment priorities

Your clinician will ask about your medical history, current concerns, and treatments you’ve tried. Be ready to discuss diagnoses, allergies, surgeries, medications, supplements, and any previous use of peptides or hormone therapy. Details that seem unrelated may still help the clinician assess your situation, so share them openly.

You’ll also have a chance to explain what brought you to the consultation and what you’d like help with. A [peptide consultation](https://innovative-care.com/peptide-consultation) should consider your individual health history and needs, not rely on a one-size-fits-all recommendation. If you’re unsure whether something is relevant, mention it and let the clinician decide. You can also bring questions or notes so you don’t forget anything during the appointment.

### Discuss your goals, lifestyle, and realistic expectations

Tell the clinician what you hope to address, such as changes in energy, recovery, body composition, or another health concern. They may ask about your sleep, exercise, nutrition, stress, and daily routine. Be specific about what you’ve noticed, when it started, and how it affects your everyday life.

Use the conversation to ask what is known about the options being discussed, what remains uncertain, and how you would assess whether a plan is helping. No treatment can guarantee a particular result, and peptides may not be the right next step. A [consultation discussion](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) can cover your goals, medications, possible risks, follow-up, and realistic expectations. Sharing what you hope to achieve helps the clinician explain what may be reasonable for your situation.

### For telehealth, ask how the clinician will assess your identity, health information, and needs

Before your appointment, ask how the telehealth platform confirms your identity and collects the information the clinician needs. You may be asked to provide basic details, a health history, and relevant records. Find out how to submit documents, what technology you’ll need, and how your health information is handled.

The appointment should give you time to describe your concerns, ask questions, and understand the clinician’s recommendations. If you’re new to telehealth, say so and ask the clinician to explain how the visit will work. You can also check how to contact the care team if you have trouble connecting or need to follow up. If you’re unsure whether the clinician has the information needed to assess your needs remotely, ask what else they require before discussing potential options.

### Find out whether telehealth is appropriate or you need an in-person evaluation

Telehealth can make it easier to speak with a clinician, but it may not be suitable for every concern. Ask whether your symptoms, health history, or potential treatment require an in-person exam or another type of evaluation. The clinician may need more information before deciding what options could be appropriate.

If they recommend an in-person visit, ask what it would help assess and whether you should contact your primary care clinician or a specialist. A responsible consultation should explain the limits of remote care rather than assume every concern can be evaluated online. Treatment recommendations should be personalized and based on a clinician’s review of your circumstances, as described in this overview of [peptide consultations](https://innovative-care.com/peptide-consultation). You can ask what the next step would involve before arranging it.

### Discuss possible options without assuming you’ll receive a prescription or treatment

Approach the consultation as a conversation about your health, not a promise of a prescription. Depending on your needs and medical history, the clinician may discuss peptide therapy, other approaches, further evaluation, or no treatment. Ask what supports each option and what risks or uncertainties to consider. If you’ve heard a claim about a peptide, bring it up and ask the clinician to explain what is known.

You don’t have to make a decision during the appointment. Take time to consider the information and ask follow-up questions if anything is unclear. You might discuss potential benefits, side effects, dosing, monitoring, and alternatives, as outlined in these [questions to discuss with a clinician](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides). The clinician will use their medical judgment to determine whether a treatment is appropriate for you.

### Identify any records, tests, or evaluations you may need before a decision

The clinician may need more information before recommending a next step. Depending on your health history and concerns, they might ask for medical records, recent lab results, or an additional evaluation. Ask what information is needed, why it matters, and whether to arrange it through the telehealth provider, your primary care clinician, or another professional.

Check with the clinician before ordering tests yourself. Clarify who will arrange and review any testing, how you’ll receive the results, and how those results could affect the plan. A consultation may help determine whether treatment is appropriate or whether another step should come first. If an evaluation is recommended, ask whether the clinician will wait for the results before making a treatment recommendation. You can also ask when to expect an update and whom to contact with questions.

## Will You Need Evaluations or Lab Tests?

### Testing depends on your health history, symptoms, and potential treatment

There isn’t one set of lab tests for everyone considering peptide therapy. A clinician may recommend an evaluation based on your health history, symptoms, medications, and the specific treatment being discussed. They may also need more information before deciding whether a treatment is appropriate. As a result, tests recommended for one person may not be relevant to someone else.

Gather any recent results you already have, but don’t order tests on your own unless a clinician asks you to. Ask what information they need and whether your existing records are enough. [Testing recommendations](https://innovative-care.com/peptide-consultation) can depend on individual circumstances, so an online lab panel may not be right for you.

### Ask why the clinician recommends each test and what it can and cannot show

If a clinician recommends testing, ask what each test is intended to assess and how the result relates to your symptoms or the treatment being discussed. You can also ask about its limits. For example, can it help identify a suspected issue, rule something out, or provide just one part of a broader health picture? A lab result needs to be considered alongside other information, not treated as a diagnosis on its own.

Understanding the purpose of each test can help you decide whether it makes sense for your situation. Ask whether the result could affect the care plan, or whether another kind of evaluation may be more useful. ActiveMed Health recommends discussing the [purpose and relevance of testing](https://activemedhealth.com/peptide-therapy-consultation) during a peptide therapy consultation.

### Clarify how results may affect your eligibility, treatment, or follow-up

Before agreeing to testing, ask how different results could affect the next steps. Could a result lead the clinician to request more information, discuss another approach, or decide that a treatment may not be suitable? Ask whether they’ll explain the findings in plain language and review your options with you before making a treatment decision.

It’s also useful to ask whether results could mean repeat testing or another evaluation. Testing doesn’t guarantee that you’ll be eligible for treatment or receive a prescription. Your clinician should consider the results alongside your health history, symptoms, and individual needs. Ask how the findings may shape your care plan and what alternatives could be considered, as [Dr. Sobo’s guidance](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) recommends discussing with your clinician.

### Ask who will review and share your results, and whether you may need further evaluation

Find out who will review your results, how you’ll receive them, and whom to contact with questions. If you’re using telehealth, ask how results will be shared and whether you’ll have a follow-up appointment to discuss them. You can also ask how long results typically take and what happens if a finding needs prompt attention.

Ask whether the clinician might request additional records, repeat a test, or recommend an in-person evaluation before making a decision. These steps can help them assess your situation; they don’t guarantee that treatment will be offered or ruled out. The [AAMC’s questions about peptide care](https://www.aamc.org/news/10-questions-ask-your-doctor-about-peptides) include asking who will interpret results and whether further evaluation may be needed.

## How Do Clinicians Assess Evidence, Benefits, and Safety?

A thoughtful consultation should focus on the specific peptide being considered, the reason for considering it, and your health history. Ask what is known, what remains uncertain, and how your clinician would monitor your health if treatment is appropriate. A consultation is a chance to weigh options, not a promise that you will receive a prescription.

### Ask what research supports the specific peptide and intended use

Ask your clinician to name the exact peptide and explain why it may be relevant to your concern. Evidence for one peptide or medical use does not automatically apply to another. Ask whether studies involved people with a health profile like yours, what outcomes they measured, and whether those outcomes relate to your goals. It is also fair to ask whether another treatment has stronger evidence. A clear answer should separate findings from research, clinical experience, and marketing claims. ActiveMed Health offers a useful starting point in its [guidance on peptide therapy consultations](https://activemedhealth.com/peptide-therapy-consultation), which recommends asking what product is proposed, why it is being considered, and what evidence supports that use.

### Discuss the evidence’s quality, limits, and unanswered questions

Ask what types of studies support the proposed treatment. A small or early-stage study can suggest a direction for future research, but it does not provide the same confidence as larger, well-designed clinical trials. Find out whether the research was published in a peer-reviewed journal, whether other researchers have confirmed the findings, and what questions remain open. Evidence for some peptides promoted for wellness or performance is limited, and it may not support every advertised use. The [American Association of Medical Colleges](https://www.aamc.org/news/10-questions-ask-your-doctor-about-peptides) notes that evidence for the clinical use of some peptides is lacking. Your clinician should explain uncertainty plainly and help you understand how it affects a treatment decision.

### Consider potential benefits without treating early research or marketing claims as proven

Ask which benefits researchers are studying, while keeping potential effects separate from established results. Claims about muscle growth, fat loss, recovery, energy, or longevity may go beyond the evidence, particularly when they rely on testimonials or promotional material. Ask what outcomes are supported for the specific peptide and intended use, how likely improvement may be, and how you would measure it. Discuss what you would do if you do not notice a benefit. Eric Topol has questioned the evidence behind many popular peptide claims in his [review of the peptide trend](https://erictopol.substack.com/p/the-peptide-craze). A responsible discussion should set realistic expectations and avoid promising a particular result. Your clinician can also help compare the potential benefits with other options, including approaches that do not involve medication.

### Review side effects, allergies, medication interactions, and reasons treatment may not suit you

Share a complete list of your allergies, medications, supplements, and relevant health conditions. Ask about known side effects, possible interactions, and health circumstances that could make a treatment unsuitable. Risks can vary by peptide, dose, route, formulation, and individual health history. Ask which risks are well understood and which remain uncertain because research is limited for the proposed use. Find out what symptoms to watch for and whom to contact if they occur. Some injectable products may cause injection-site or allergic reactions; [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/medications-and-treatments/peptides-what-they-are-potential-benefits-and-safety-concerns) notes that severe allergic reactions are possible with some injectable peptides. Do not use online dosing advice to manage a reaction or change a prescribed treatment.

### Ask whether the medication is FDA-approved for its intended use or compounded, and what that means for evidence and oversight

Ask whether the exact product is FDA-approved for the condition or purpose being discussed. A product’s approval for one use does not mean it is approved for every use. Compounded medications are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review them for safety and effectiveness before they are marketed. Compounding may meet specific patient needs, but it is not a substitute for evidence supporting the intended use. Ask why a compounded medication is being considered, what standards govern its preparation, and what is known about its use for your situation. The FDA explains the [difference between approved and compounded drugs](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers). Your clinician should make these distinctions clear so you can weigh the potential benefits, limits, and uncertainties.

### Ask about the product’s source, formulation, and quality controls

Ask who supplies or prepares the medication, where it will be dispensed, and how its identity, strength, and quality are checked. If it is compounded, find out which pharmacy prepares it and how it should be stored and shipped. Check that the packaging includes clear instructions and contact information for questions. Products from unverified online sellers may have unreliable sourcing or quality controls, so ask how the proposed product can be traced to a legitimate pharmacy or supplier. Compounded medications do not undergo the FDA’s premarket review for safety and effectiveness that applies to FDA-approved drugs, as described in the agency’s [information about drug compounding](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers). A clinician should be able to explain the product’s formulation and the safeguards used to prepare and dispense it.

### If you compete in sports, check whether your governing organization prohibits the substance

If you take part in organized sports, check the rules before considering a peptide. Prohibited-substance lists can vary by sport, league, and competition level. A substance may be restricted even if it is prescribed or promoted for wellness. Ask your clinician for the medication’s exact name and any alternate names, then confirm its status with your team, league, or governing organization. The [World Anti-Doping Agency’s Prohibited List](https://www.wada-ama.org/en/prohibited-list) is one reference, but your organization may follow additional rules. Do not assume a product is permitted because a clinic or pharmacy offers it. If the status is unclear, contact your sports organization before starting treatment and ask whether you need documentation or a therapeutic use exemption.

### Learn which symptoms or reactions need prompt medical attention

Before starting, ask which side effects you can monitor at home and which need prompt medical attention. Confirm whom to contact during office hours and where to seek help after hours. Trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, faintness, or rapidly worsening symptoms after taking or injecting a medication may signal a severe allergic reaction. Seek emergency care if these occur. [MedlinePlus explains common signs of anaphylaxis](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000844.htm) and why it requires urgent attention. For less severe but concerning changes, such as persistent pain, a rash, or new symptoms, contact your clinician for guidance. Do not change the dose or stop and restart treatment on your own. Keep the medication packaging available if you seek care, as it can help clinicians identify the product and its ingredients.

## What Should You Ask Before Considering Treatment?

A consultation is a chance to understand your options, not a commitment to start treatment. Ask about the specific peptide being discussed, why it may suit your situation, and what evidence and follow-up support the plan. Take notes, and ask the clinician to explain anything that is unclear.

### Why might this option—or another approach—fit my health history and goals?

Ask how your health history and goals relate to the treatment being discussed. Share relevant diagnoses, allergies, medications, supplements, previous treatments, and any side effects you have experienced. Be specific about what you hope to address, then ask how the peptide is expected to relate to that goal and what factors might make it unsuitable for you.

A careful discussion should cover your medical history, relevant lab results, possible side effects, dosing, follow-up, and realistic expectations. [Dr. Sobo’s guide to discussing peptides with a doctor](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) offers a helpful list of topics to raise. Ask the clinician to explain the recommendation in plain language, including what is known and what remains uncertain.

### What alternatives, including no treatment, should I consider?

Ask what other options could address your concern. Depending on your circumstances, those might include an established treatment, changes to sleep or training habits, further evaluation, or monitoring without starting treatment. Ask what you might expect if you wait or decide not to pursue treatment. Knowing the possible trade-offs can help you choose an approach that fits your needs without feeling rushed.

The right option depends on your goals, health history, medications, risk factors, and previous treatment experiences. [Dr. Sobo’s consultation guide](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) recommends reviewing these factors before considering peptide therapy. Ask why the clinician favors one approach over another, what alternatives may be appropriate for your concern, and whether you can take time to review the information before deciding.

### What benefits are realistic, and how will we assess whether treatment helps?

Ask what outcome the treatment is intended to support and what evidence applies to that specific peptide and use. Broad claims about muscle growth, fat loss, recovery, or wellness do not guarantee results. Evidence for one peptide does not establish that another will have the same effects, and research findings may not predict your individual response. Ask the clinician to explain the evidence, its limitations, and any unanswered questions.

If treatment is considered, agree on how you will assess whether it is helping. You might track symptoms, function, or other measures selected with your clinician. Ask when you will check in, what would count as meaningful progress, and what might prompt a change in plan. When reviewing [reports about peptide therapy outcomes](https://www.peptidejournal.org/lifestyle/peptide-therapy-success-stories-what-works), ask your clinician how those claims compare with evidence for your specific situation.

### How long might treatment last, and what will it cost? What monitoring and follow-up will I need?

Ask what the proposed schedule would involve, how long treatment might continue, and how the clinician would decide whether to continue, adjust, or stop it. There may not be one duration that suits everyone. Request a clear follow-up plan, including any appointments, lab tests, or other checks, and ask who will review the results with you.

Discuss the full cost before deciding. Ask about the consultation, medication, testing, follow-up visits, and any other expected expenses. Confirm what is included and whether costs could change if your care plan changes. [Innovative Care’s peptide consultation overview](https://innovative-care.com/peptide-consultation) notes that treatment options should take your health history, goals, medications, and applicable regulations into account. Ask for time to review the plan and costs before agreeing.

### Who should I contact about side effects, concerns, or health changes?

Before starting treatment, ask who to contact and how to reach the care team. Find out how to report a side effect, ask a nonurgent question, or share a change in your health. Ask which symptoms need prompt medical attention and where to seek help if the clinic is unavailable. If you have a primary care clinician or other specialists, ask how they can be kept informed about treatment and follow-up.

Confirm the exact product being discussed, why it is being considered for your concern, and what evidence supports that use. [ActiveMed Health’s consultation guidance](https://activemedhealth.com/peptide-therapy-consultation) highlights these as useful questions to raise. Request written instructions for use and storage, along with details about monitoring. If the product, instructions, or expected effects are unclear, ask the clinician to explain before proceeding.

## What Happens After the Consultation?

The consultation is the start of a clinical review, not a guarantee that you’ll receive a prescription or begin treatment. Your clinician may need more information, or may recommend a different next step based on your health and goals. Before leaving the appointment, make sure you know what happens next, who will contact you, and how to ask follow-up questions.

### Your clinician may request records, tests, or another evaluation before deciding

Your clinician may need more information before making a treatment decision. They might ask to review relevant medical records, recommend lab work, or suggest another evaluation. This can help them better understand your health history and assess whether a potential treatment is appropriate for you.

Ask what each request is meant to clarify, how the results could affect your options, and when you can expect an update. Don’t arrange tests on your own unless your clinician asks you to. [Dr. Sobo’s guide to discussing peptides with a doctor](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) also notes that additional records or evaluations may be needed before a clinician can decide.

### Your clinician will base treatment decisions on clinical judgment and your individual suitability

Your clinician should consider your health history, symptoms, goals, and the available evidence before discussing treatment options. A peptide you ask about may not be suitable for you, and a consultation does not guarantee a prescription. Your clinician may recommend another approach, request more information, or advise against treatment.

Ask how a recommendation relates to your circumstances, what uncertainties remain, and what alternatives you could consider, including no treatment. A [peptide consultation overview from Innovative Care](https://innovative-care.com/peptide-consultation) describes treatment decisions as individualized and guided by clinical judgment. Take time to understand the reasoning and raise any concerns before deciding on next steps.

### Before starting, review how to use and store the treatment, plus monitoring and follow-up

If your clinician recommends treatment, make sure you understand the instructions before you begin. Ask how to use and store it, what to do if you miss a dose, and whom to contact with questions. Confirm when follow-up will happen and how your clinician plans to assess your response, review any test results, or address side effects.

Keep the instructions handy, and ask for clarification if anything is unclear. Don’t change how you use the treatment without checking with your clinician. [Dr. Sobo’s guidance on peptide therapy](https://drsobo.com/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor-about-peptides) recommends reviewing use, storage, monitoring, and follow-up before starting.

### Keep your primary care clinician informed about treatment and new symptoms

Tell your primary care clinician if you start treatment. Share the name of the medication, the dose, and the name of the prescribing clinician when you can. This gives your care team a clearer picture of your treatment plan and helps them consider it alongside your other medications and health conditions.

If you notice new symptoms or changes in how you feel, contact your prescribing clinician and let your primary care clinician know. Ask in advance who to contact first and which symptoms need prompt attention. The [American Association of Medical Colleges’ guidance on questions to ask about peptides](https://www.aamc.org/news/10-questions-ask-your-doctor-about-peptides) also recommends keeping your primary care clinician informed about peptide use and new symptoms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a peptide therapy consultation mean I’ll receive a prescription?**\
No. A consultation helps a clinician review your health history, concerns, and goals. They may discuss treatment, suggest more evaluation, recommend another approach, or decide that peptide therapy isn’t appropriate.

**Should I order lab tests before my appointment?**\
Usually, you don’t need to order tests unless a clinician asks you to. Bring any recent results you already have, along with a list of your medications, supplements, allergies, and relevant health conditions.

**Can a telehealth clinician assess whether peptide therapy is right for me?**\
A clinician can review your information remotely, but telehealth may not be suitable for every concern. They may request records or tests, or recommend an in-person evaluation before discussing treatment.

**Are compounded peptides the same as FDA-approved medications?**\
No. Compounded medications are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review them for safety and effectiveness before they are marketed. Ask why a compounded product is being considered and what is known about its use for your specific situation.

**What should I ask if a peptide is recommended?**\
Ask what the peptide is intended to address, what evidence supports that use, what risks and alternatives to consider, and how follow-up would work. Confirm the product’s source, cost, instructions, and who to contact with questions or side effects.
